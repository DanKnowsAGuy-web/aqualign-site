+31%

Corn: Smith Farms, Utopia, Ontario

2012 · open field · non-irrigated · single treatment at planting

The test. One of the hottest, driest growing seasons in a decade. Treated and untreated non-GMO, non-Roundup-Ready seed was planted with the same seed drill in the same unirrigated field: fertilized once, herbicide applied once, then left to the weather. AquAlign was applied exactly once, at planting. Growth was monitored from seeding in the week of May 20 until harvest in the last week of October, with samples taken from the field's sandy-loam sections.

What was measured. Speed and quality of germination, evidenced by root and leaf growth, then yield at harvest.

The results. By day 14, treated plants averaged 12.4 inches against 7.3 for control, with roughly 50% thicker stalks at the base and visibly stronger root growth. By late July: 7.1 feet versus 5.6. At harvest, the treated rows delivered more, larger, heavier, kernel-dense cobs: a net yield 31% above control.

"In one of the hottest, driest years I can recall, from sprout through to harvest, the AquAlign results were significantly and measurably better."

Tom Smith, Smith Farms; named one of the 100 Most Influential Canadians in Agriculture of the 20th century

Afterward: Smith Farms extended AquAlign across all its corn fields and soybean plantings the following season. An August photo of an average soy plant (non-GMO, minimal chemical intervention) showed over 21 pods on a single typical plant.

Greenhouse tomatoes: Van Hart Greenhouses, Holland Marsh, Ontario

2012 · commercial organic greenhouse · nutrient irrigation manifold

The operation. Certified organic since the 1980s, Van Hart Greenhouses has developed 45 vegetable varieties and experimented with over 300 tomato varietals. Its computerized nutrient irrigation system produces some of the highest per-plant yields recorded, at a cost: plants are often completely exhausted by mid-season, forcing removal and a 4 to 5 week replanting gap.

The test. One greenhouse's irrigation manifold received AquAlign; a second house planted with the same varietal served as control. Because this was a working commercial operation, owner Ron Van Hart reserved the right to end the trial if anything went wrong. The summer of 2012 was hotter and drier than normal, with higher-than-usual insect pressure.

The results. The control planting was exhausted and removed on schedule, July 16, after 38 days of production. The AquAlign house kept producing fruit and flowers until the second hard frost forced removal on October 8: 121 days of continuous yield. One treated planting out-produced two control plantings. Brix samples averaged 7.68 against 6.5 for control, 15.3% higher: sweeter, denser, more nutritious fruit.

Afterward: Van Hart extended AquAlign to all his greenhouses the next season, diverted conditioned water to his field crops with what he called extremely gratifying results, and swapped part of his tomato planting for cucumbers with a similar bonus yield.

Commercial eggs: 42,000-hen flock, Canada

2013 · 8-week controlled demonstration · Canada's largest vertically integrated egg producer

The test. An 8-week controlled demonstration on a flock of 42,000 ISA Browns, comparing an 8-week control period (flock weeks 44 to 51) against the demonstration period (weeks 56 to 63). A mechanical failure unrelated to the system caused a partial-flock dehydration incident mid-trial; the results below include it.

The results.

  • Water consumption down 259 gallons per day: 14,504 gallons less over the demonstration period, an 11% reduction, on better-hydrated birds.
  • The rate of age-related production decline was reduced by 27%.
  • Average egg weight of 62.2 g through the demonstration; the ideal-weight criteria held to the end of the flock's cycle (week 72: 63.2 g).
  • Improved egg quality: fewer deformed eggs and floaters.
  • Manure came out significantly drier with sharply reduced free ammonia: fewer flies, healthier birds, easier storage, and premium resale value.

"We sell 90,000 tons of manure a year and at even an additional dollar per ton, that more than pays for the AquAlign systems, before the benefits delivered to our primary egg revenue."

Operations manager

Afterward: the operator asked to keep the system in place through the flock's full production cycle, then purchased it. A veterinary observer on a companion egg demonstration noted "a low cost, high quality, culture negative egg… achieved without the use of systemic antibiotics."

Alfalfa: germination, growth & drought resilience

2012 · controlled demonstration · water only, no nutrients

Why alfalfa. It is the world's most cultivated forage legume (a 2006 estimate put production at 436 million tons), and stand establishment is its make-or-break phase.

The test. Two identical trays lined with paper-towel substrate, seeded with equal measures of certified-organic seed from a single lot. All water came from one municipal source, dual-filtered; one beaker was conditioned with AquAlign, the other left untreated. Each tray could receive at most 118 ml per day, water only, no nutrients. Ten days of growth, then a five-day (120-hour) total water cutoff to simulate drought stress.

The results. By day 10: 98% germination versus 80%, and average height of 44 mm versus 20 mm, market-ready sprouts against barely-started controls, a 54.5% faster growth rate. After 120 hours without water, the control tray suffered 25% die-off and roughly 39% die-back; the conditioned tray had zero die-off and only minor leaf fade. When watering resumed, conditioned plants recovered fully within two days; control die-off continued past 50%.

Companion field demonstration: a year-long trial under center-pivot irrigation over 7-year-old plants at K-C Farms, Colorado. The owner recorded brix readings of 23 in a test area against 10 in a second test and 7 in control: "I have never seen a brix reading over 15 here." He also noted faster bloom, projecting the possibility of one more cutting per year.

Celery: Horling Farms

2012–13 · commercial seeding line · standard seed

The challenge. Celery seed is notoriously hard to germinate commercially. Premium germination-enhanced seed exists, but the owner of Horling Farms rarely found the annual cost-benefit worthwhile.

The test. One skid (180 flats of 338 cells each) was seeded through the automated line with conditioned water; the control skid ran the following day on untreated water to rule out residual treatment in the system. A conditioning unit was also installed on the spray irrigation for the greenhouse quadrant holding the treated flats. One complication worked against the trial: the treated flats' greenhouse averaged 6°F hotter than the control house, above celery's optimal range, and heat directly suppresses celery germination.

The results. Despite the temperature handicap, treated flats germinated 11% better overall. Comparing the two flats closest to optimal temperature, the treated flat germinated 40% better than its control. The grower's documented takeaway: a floor of 11% improvement using standard seed, with more expected under normal conditions.

Tobacco: Dirse Farms, Walsingham, Ontario

2012 · open field · seedling irrigation only

The test. Tobacco is a transplanted seedling crop, so conditioned water was used only in the greenhouse irrigation of the starter plants; nothing was treated in the field. The goal was faster maturity, after frost losses in prior years.

The results. Treated plants reached harvest a full 14 days ahead of the control field. An early frost then hit the control field with only 25% of it harvested, exactly the risk the grower was trying to beat. Measured against that harvested portion, treated plants yielded 30% more, and graded Choice Quality, tobacco's top tier, selling at top prices.

"Bringing the crop in 2 weeks before the frost hit, with a grading of Choice Quality, made all the difference to our year's bottom-line."

Dirse Farms

Read-across: tomato, pepper, and potato share tobacco's plant family; the trial was designed so its findings extend to those crops.

Kenaf: plant & root development, Phoenix, Arizona

2012 · controlled study · 31 days at 90–105°F

The question. Kenaf grows fast, and it was chosen to answer a pointed question: does faster growth come at the expense of the root system?

The test. Four pot plantings in desert soil mixed 20% with mulch, six seeds each, 200 ml of water daily, 31 days of Arizona summer (July 24 to August 24, average daily temperatures 90 to 105°F). Two variables were crossed: dry-planted versus pre-soaked seed, and conditioned versus municipal water. At the end, every plant was unearthed and both plants and rootballs measured.

Averaged measurements at day 31
PlantingHeightRootball
Control 1 (dry seed, tap water)10.25"2.4" × 2.4"
Control 2 (soaked, tap water)9.75"2.5" × 3.5"
Treated 1 (dry seed, conditioned)10.25"3.0" × 3.2"
Treated 2 (soaked, conditioned)11.25"4.0" × 3.9"

The results. Dry-seed comparison: equal height, 40% greater rootball. Soaked-seed comparison: 13.4% taller with a 43.9% larger rootball. Bigger roots and equal-or-better tops, the opposite of a trade-off, and the trait behind the drought resilience seen in the alfalfa study.

A note on honesty

These are demonstrations and commercial trials, not peer-reviewed studies. Where controls were compromised (frost, heat differentials, equipment failures) we say so above, because that's what real growing seasons do. Field conditions vary and we don't promise a specific number: across documented trials, primary-measure improvements ranged from 11% to 31%, and our working expectation for a new installation is a 10%+ improvement. We'd rather under-promise and show you the data.
